The Ten Slots

Read the ad. Pick its shape. Then ask the same ten kinds of questions, in the same order, every time. Each slot is one kind of question. Ten slots give you the "around ten questions" the exam asks for. This plan was tested on all 78 practice ads. It works for every one. This page tells you what to ask. sheet_A tells you how to say it in French.

Pocket card — the whole plan

1. Pick the shape. Ask yourself: who moves? Which way does the money go? → GO · COME · TAKE · FAR · FLIP
2. Go through the ten slots in order. Spend about 30 seconds on each. Skip the two that fit this ad least:

  1. WHAT exactly? (ask about every vague line in the ad)
  2. FOR WHOM? (age · level · group · licence)
  3. WHEN? (days · season · how long · how often)
  4. WHERE? (place · how to get there · area served)
  5. WITH WHOM? (staff · training · people per teacher)
  6. HOW MUCH? (price · what it includes · discounts · deposit)
  7. WITH WHAT? (what they provide · what I bring or send)
  8. WHAT IF? (weather · cancel · damage · refund)
  9. HOW DO I START? (steps · how far ahead · trial · papers)
  10. WHAT ELSE? (food · a place to stay · extras · gifts)

3. Mind blank? Point at a line in the ad · repeat a fact and ask for more · invent one detail about yourself.

Node 1Pick the shape

This step takes two seconds. Ask yourself one question: who moves, and which way does the money go? Some ads mix two shapes — guitar lessons at your home, or a boat rental with a guide you can add. Pick the shape that fits most of the ad. The ten slots will cover the rest.

GOI go to them and do / see / learn something47 of 78 · 60%
COMEThey come to me, or do it for me13 of 78 · 17%
TAKEI rent a thing and use it myself7 of 78 · 9%
FLIPThey pay me, or no money changes hands6 of 78 · 8%
FARI order from far away; it ships to me or happens online5 of 78 · 6%

Node 2The ten slots, in order

Use the same order for every ad, with no exceptions. A fixed order means you never have to wonder what comes next. Each slot is a kind of question, not exact words — you make up the real question on the spot. Tap a shape button below. The example line in each slot will change to match that shape.

1
WHAT exactly?
Ask what the offer really includes. Every vague line in the ad gives you one easy question.
2
FOR WHOM?
Ask if it works for you: your age, your level, your experience, your group size, a licence, your fitness.
3
WHEN?
Ask about days, hours, the season, how long it lasts, how often it runs, and deadlines.
4
WHERE?
Ask about the address, how to get there, parking, the area they serve, and indoors or outdoors.
5
WITH WHOM?
Ask who runs it and who shows up: their training, their experience, people per teacher, and whether it is the same person each time.
6
HOW MUCH?
Ask the price, what it includes, discounts, the deposit, and how to pay.
7
WITH WHAT?
Ask about equipment. Do they provide it, or do I bring, wear, or send something?
8
WHAT IF?
Ask about problems: safety, bad weather, cancelling, refunds, damage, and guarantees.
9
HOW DO I START?
Ask how to sign up: the steps, how far ahead, a trial, a visit, and the papers you need.
10
WHAT ELSE?
Ask about extras: food, a place to stay, a shop, photos, private bookings, pets, and gifts.

The math: the call lasts about 5 minutes. That is about 10 questions, or 30 seconds per slot. On a short ad, slots 5 and 7 are usually the ones to skip. Slots 1, 3, 6, 8, 9, and 10 work for every one of the 78 ads. Skip two slots, ask one extra follow-up question in each of your two best slots, and you still reach ten questions.

When your mind goes blank

Sometimes a slot feels empty and you freeze. These three tricks work on every one of the 78 ads:

1 · Point at a line in the ad. Read a vague line back to them. Then ask what it means, exactly.
"The ad says 'varied missions' — what kind of missions, exactly?" Every ad has 3 to 6 vague lines. Each one gives you a free question.
2 · Repeat a fact, then ask for more. Say a fact from the ad back to them. Then ask a question about it.
"The ad says with or without a guide — what does the guide add, and how much extra does it cost?"
3 · Invent one detail about yourself. Before you call, make up ONE fact about your life: two kids, total beginner, free only on Saturdays, no car. Use that fact in slots 2, 3, and 4.
"I would come with my two children, aged six and nine — is that possible?"

The Ten Moves

In Section B you call a friend about an ad and try to get them to join. The friend will object. Listen to each objection, decide which of the 8 types it is, and answer it with one of the 10 moves on this page. The page uses all 81 Section B practice prompts, each counted the same. It is the partner page of The Ten Slots: there you ask a stranger questions, here you talk a friend into coming.

The reframe — read this first

Why do we want to say "then don't do it"? Because we do not care about the answer. If a friend says no, and their no costs you nothing, "okay, then don't" is the natural reply. So before you speak, decide why you care.

In Section B, you need the friend to say yes. Pick your reason during the 20-second prep. There are three to choose from: you want company, you think this will help them, or the group needs people. Once you have a reason, their "no" is no longer just their business. It is a problem you want to solve. The moves below show you how to solve it.

One more thing: each objection helps you. It tells you which move to use next. A friend who said yes right away would leave you with nothing to say for nine minutes.

Pocket card — the whole plan

Prep (20 seconds): TRAIT (find the friend's need in the task line) → STAKE (pick your reason: with me / for you / for it) → AMMO (pick 3 lines from the ad that answer objections before they come).

The call (5 beats): HOOK → SHOW the ad → MATCH it to their need → VOLLEY their objections → LOCK the next step.

The VOLLEY loop: hear the objection → name its type (TIME · MONEY · ABILITY · FEAR · TRUST · EFFORT · WANT · BOND) → answer with 2 moves → ask a short question back. Never use 4 moves in a row. That is talking at them without listening.

Node 0Prep — 20 seconds before you speak

  1. TRAIT
    In 78 of the 81 prompts, the task line tells you what the friend needs ("loves animals", "needs money", "very shy", "always on screens"). This is what you aim your pitch at. If the prompt does not give one, or it does not fit, pick one yourself and say it in your first line.
  2. STAKE
    Ask yourself: why do I want them to come? Pick one of three reasons: WITH ME (I want to go, but not alone) · FOR YOU (I think this will help you) · FOR IT (the group or cause needs people).
  3. AMMO
    Pick 3 lines from the ad that already answer likely objections: "beginners accepted", "free trial", "maximum safety", "flexible hours", "2,000 volunteers". Almost every ad has lines like these. They are ready-made answers.

SkeletonThe call — 5 beats, 10 minutes

  1. HOOK · 30 s
    Say hello, give your reason, and name their need: "I saw an ad and thought of you right away — you said you wanted…"
  2. SHOW · 1–2 min
    Tell them what the ad says: what it is, when, where, and how much it costs. These are slots 1 / 3 / 4 / 6 from The Ten Slots, but now you tell instead of ask.
  3. MATCH · 1 min
    Give two or three reasons this fits them. Talk about their need, not just how good the offer is.
  4. VOLLEY · 5–6 min
    They object. Name the type (one of 8). Answer with 2 moves. Then ask a short question back ("qu'est-ce que t'en dis ?"). This repeats 3 to 5 times.
  5. LOCK · 30 s
    End with a clear next step and a date: "I will send you the link tonight. We sign up on Saturday."

TEF · Expression écrite · Section A

The Six Beats

You get the start of a short news story. You must continue it in 100–200 words. This page gives you one method. First, decide what the last line promised. Then write the same six beats, in the same order, every time. Each beat uses one French verb form the exam grades. The method works on all 72 openers. This page covers only what to write — the French itself is up to you. It is the partner of The Ten Slots and The Ten Moves: those pages are for speaking, this one is for writing.

The main idea — read this first

The last line of every opener promises something. "He discovered something extraordinary…" — "she found an original solution…" — "but on his return…". The story stops right before the good part. Think of that promise as a debt. Your story must pay it back.

So you never start from nothing. The prompt already made the hardest choice for you. Your job is simple: name the debt, then pay it in Beat 4, and build the other five beats around it. That is the whole task. There is no blank page, because the story is already started.

Pocket card — the whole method

1 · Pick the debt: danger? → SAVE · someone doing wrong? → FALL · an "original way" promised? → WAY · a hidden thing to name? → THING · none of these → STIR. Take the first one that fits.

2 · Write the six beats, 1–2 sentences each, about 25 words:

  1. PICK UP — continue from the last word, move one step ahead · passé composé
  2. FREEZE — describe that moment: place, time, faces · imparfait
  3. REWIND — tell what had happened before · plus-que-parfait
  4. PAY THE DEBT — give what the last line promised · PC + direct speech
  5. RIPPLE — show how people react: witnesses, police, news · passive
  6. SETTLE — end the story, add one last line · conditionnel passé + subjonctif passé

3 · Paragraphs: 1+2 | 3+4 | 5 | 6. Short on time? Write beats 1+4+5+6 only — about 100 words. Never skip Beat 4.

4 · Stuck? Pick from the four answers (money · animal · paper · a face from the past) · add a witness · reuse the opener's details.

Node 1Name the debt

Ask one question: what did the last line promise? Check the five types in this order. Take the first one that fits. If you pick the wrong debt, it costs you little — the six beats stay the same. Only what you write in Beat 4 changes.

SAVESomeone is in danger → write the rescue8 of 72 · 11%
FALLSomeone is doing wrong → write how they get caught10 of 72 · 14%
WAYAn "original solution / idea / reaction" is promised → invent the clever plan15 of 72 · 21%
THINGSomething hidden — an object, secret, person, or cause → say exactly what it is27 of 72 · 38%
STIRNone of the above — a wild scene is going on → show how it calms down12 of 72 · 17%

Node 2Write the six beats

Write the beats in this order every time, for every opener. The fixed order means you never have to think about structure. Each beat holds one kind of content and has one main tense. Together, the six tenses cover every form the exam grades. Tap a debt type below. The six beats will change to match it:

1
PICK UP · passé composé
Start where the opener's last word stopped. Push the action one step forward. Do not restart the story or repeat the prompt.
2
FREEZE · imparfait
Describe that exact moment: the place, the time, the faces, the weather.
3
REWIND · plus-que-parfait
Tell the backstory: what had happened before this moment.
4
PAY THE DEBT · passé composé + direct speech
Give exactly what the last line promised. This is the most important beat.
5
RIPPLE · passive
Show how others react: witnesses, family, police, the news.
6
SETTLE · conditionnel passé + subjonctif passé
End the story. Give the outcome, then add one last line: a quote, a lesson, or a small twist.

The task sheet asks for 100–200 words (at least 80) in 3–4 paragraphs. Six beats at 20–30 words each gives 120–180 words. That fits with room to spare. Paragraphs: 1+2 | 3+4 | 5 | 6. You can also join the last two and make 3 paragraphs. If you run out of time, write beats 1+4+5+6 only. That is about 100 words and still a complete story. Never skip Beat 4. Skip at most one of Beats 2 and 3 — they carry the imparfait and the plus-que-parfait. Put a linking word where one beat ends and the next starts: en réalité before 3, c'est alors que before 4, aussitôt / par la suite before 5, finalement / néanmoins before 6.

If you get stuck

Sometimes no idea comes and you freeze. These three helpers work on every one of the 72 openers:

1 · The four answers: MONEY · ANIMAL · PAPER · A FACE FROM THE PAST. Every debt in all 72 openers can be paid with one of these four.
Sofa → banknotes. Bottle in the Congo river → a letter. Visitor on the balcony → a peacock. Veterans' meeting → the friend they thought was dead. Coffee-machine box → a snake. Do not try to be original. Pick one of the four and make it exact.
2 · Add a witness. If a paragraph stalls, add a person who saw it — a neighbor, a passer-by, a security guard. Let them do exactly one thing: gasp, film it, call someone, or say one line.
This fills Beat 5, and it gives you a free passive: «la scène a été filmée par un voisin.»
3 · Reuse the opener. Every name, age, place, and number in the prompt gives you a free sentence. Repeat it, describe it more, or show its opposite.
«À 13 ans, Walid…» — the opener gives you a third of your material, and the examiner already approved it.

Worked examples

Sofa treasure (rank 1) — THING
  1. PICK UP He cut the lining open — and stopped cold.
  2. FREEZE It was Sunday morning, in a quiet kitchen. The dog watched from the doorway.
  3. REWIND He had bought the sofa the day before for 30 €. The old dealer had seemed strangely rushed.
  4. PAY THE DEBT Inside were bundles wrapped in plastic: 50 000 € in old bills, and an envelope with a woman's name on it.
  5. RIPPLE The police were called. The money was examined. The dealer was found. The story was printed in the local paper.
  6. SETTLE The money went back to the dealer's mother. She had hidden it before a move. The retiree got a reward. He said he never would have guessed a sofa could hold so much.
Prison escape (rank 9) — WAY, with «unfortunately» in the opener
  1. PICK UP At the end of visiting hours, one extra visitor walked toward the exit.
  2. FREEZE The visiting room was crowded. The guards were half asleep. The cameras pointed the other way.
  3. REWIND For weeks, his daughter had brought in a woman's outfit, piece by piece: a wig, a dress, glasses.
  4. PAY THE DEBT The plan was simple: change clothes in the toilets and walk out dressed as her. It worked — until the gate.
  5. RIPPLE He was recognized by the newest guard — the high heels gave away his walk. The alarm was raised. The daughter was questioned.
  6. SETTLE He got three extra months, and visits are now behind glass. Bien qu'il ait tout préparé, il aurait mieux fait d'attendre sa libération — it was only six weeks away.
Hard cases — the strangest openers, in short form

Posthumous gala, «Madam,» (rank 18) — THING: the only opener written as a letter. The debt is the untold story behind the late honor. FREEZE the gala. REWIND what Phillips had done without any reward — this is perfect for the plus-que-parfait. PAY THE DEBT by naming the injustice. RIPPLE with the public's reaction. SETTLE with what the minister promised. Keep one letter phrase («Madame, vous devez savoir que…») and write the six beats as usual.

Police raid, a small voice: « I know! » (rank 64) — THING: the hardest of the 72. The opener never says what the police were looking for. So build the case backward from the child's words. Think through REWIND in your head first, then write. The debt is what the child knew.

Nap in the car, thief drives off (rank 59) — FALL: watch the tenses. The opener slips into the present («he wakes up»). Keep your beats in past tenses. The debt is FALL, not THING, because FALL comes earlier in the check order.

Navy rescue, «did not expect their reaction» (rank 45) — SAVE: this opener has two debts — a rescue AND an unknown reaction. The check order says SAVE comes first. The reaction then becomes the surprise inside Beat 4.

The two flight-diversion openers (ranks 22 vs 67): almost the same story, but different debts. Rank 22 promises an «unusual event» to name, so it is THING. Rank 67 states the emergency directly, so it is SAVE. This shows the choice depends on the last line, not the topic. The six beats are the same either way.

TEF · Expression écrite · Section B

The Ten Lenses

You get one claim. First, name its shape — one of 5. Then write the same six-beat letter every time, about 200 words. Your three arguments come from a fixed list of ten lenses. This system was built from all 73 Section B claims, each counted equally. It is the partner of The Ten Slots and The Ten Moves — those pages are for speaking. This page plays the same game on paper.

The big idea — read this first

You do not need an opinion. You never write what you really believe. Almost every claim in the pool says too much on purpose. Look for extreme words: "destroying", "no more", "only", "all", "banned", "impossible".

Your letter does one simple job. Find the small part of the claim that is true. Agree with it. Then reject the extreme part. Do not ask "what do I think about zoos?" Ask "where does this claim say too much?" On 69 of the 73 claims, your position is the same: OUI, MAIS. Yes to the true part. No to the extreme part.

The extreme words help you. Each one shows you the exact word your letter will attack. And agreeing with the true part ("certes…") gives you your first argument for free.

Pocket card — the whole system

Prep (60 s): CIRCLE the extreme word → CALL the shape (FORCE · DOOM · VERDICT · BAN · CREED) → MINE the lenses: go down the list 1–10 and keep 3.

Letter (6 beats, about 200 words): FRAME 25 → STAND 15 → YES-BUT 45 → STRIKE 45 → BUILD 45 → SEAL 25.

Every argument: CLAIM · BECAUSE · SHOW — three sentences, about 45 words.

Stuck? Write one true sentence for the claim first · make up one plain study · zoom in to one person, or out to all of society.

Node 0Prep — 60 seconds before you write a word

  1. CIRCLE
    Circle the extreme word — «plus personne», «tous», «interdire», «inutile». That word runs your whole letter. Quote it in your STAND sentence. Aim your YES-BUT at it.
  2. CALL
    Name the shape of the claim — one of the 5 below. Each shape comes with a ready-made sentence that states your position. If the claim has two parts (a problem plus a fix), use the shape of the fix. Read "eliminate X" or "only X" as a BAN.
  3. MINE
    Go through the ten lenses in order, 1 to 10. Keep the first 3 that give you an idea, and note one more as a spare. Most claims give you 5 or 6 ideas. You only need 3.

SkeletonThe letter — 6 beats, about 200 words

  1. FRAME · ~25 w
    Greet the reader. Say where you read the claim. Then restate the claim. (The task says you must state the claim you are answering.)
  2. STAND · ~15 w
    Your position in one sentence. Use the shape's ready-made sentence. Aim it at the word you circled.
  3. YES-BUT · ~45 w
    Argument 1. One honest sentence for the claim (certes…). Then the turn (mais…). This costs you nothing, and it makes you sound fair.
  4. STRIKE · ~45 w
    Argument 2. Use your strongest lens.
  5. BUILD · ~45 w
    Argument 3. Use your second lens — or use BETTER TOOL, so you end by proposing a fix.
  6. SEAL · ~25 w
    Conclusion. Restate your "yes, but" position. Then a formal sign-off.

Word math: 25 + 15 + 45 + 45 + 45 + 25 = 200. This page counts words, not minutes. Whatever your time limit, split it about 20% prep, 70% writing, 10% rereading. In a 30-minute window, that is 6 / 21 / 3.

Node 1The shape call — what does the claim DO?

Ask one question about the claim: what does it do? The answer is one of five shapes. Each shape comes with a ready-made sentence that states your position. You never have to invent a position.

FORCEForces a rule on everyone, or gives everyone a right — "Encourage, don't impose."18 of 73 · 25%
DOOMSays something is dying or getting worse — "A change, not a death."16 of 73 · 22%
VERDICTJudges something as good, bad, or useless — "The judgment is too broad: which X, for whom?"16 of 73 · 22%
BANWants to forbid, end, or limit something — "The goal, yes — the ban, no."14 of 73 · 19%
CREEDStates a rule for how to live — "A path, not a law."9 of 73 · 12%

Tap a shape button. Its plan appears under the buttons, and the best lenses for that shape light up in the lens list below.

Node 2Mine — the ten lenses, in a fixed order

A lens is one angle to look at the claim from. Look through it and an argument appears. Pick 3 lenses and build one argument from each. Always go down the list in the same order, 1 to 10.

1FREEDOM
Who decides? Free choice versus being forced. The rights of the people the claim targets.
"A rule for everyone treats every adult like a child who cannot choose."
2FAIRNESS
The claim hits groups unequally: rich and poor, young and old, city and country.
"Why pick on this group? Who cannot follow the rule?"
3HEALTH
Body and mind: stress, addiction, well-being, pressure.
"What does it do to sleep, stress, and self-esteem?"
4BOND
Does it connect people, or does it leave them alone?
"Family meals, teams, neighbours — does the link grow or break?"
5MONEY
Cost, jobs, who pays, and what it does to the economy.
"A nice idea — but who pays, and what jobs depend on it?"
6PLANET
Good or bad for the environment. This works far beyond the ecology claims.
"Telework, food, clothes — the environment angle is almost always there."
7BACKFIRE
Would the plan even work? Side effects, ways around it, or the opposite result.
"A ban does not end the habit. It hides it."
8ELSEWHERE
Another country, or another time. What happened before? Which predictions failed?
"TV was supposed to kill cinema. It did not."
9BETTER TOOL
Propose a better fix: teach, tax, adjust, or run a small test program.
"A precise fix instead of a total one."
10ONE FACE
One real example: my cousin, my office, my street.
"My niece reads on her phone AND collects paperbacks."

Note: OUI, MAIS works on 69 of the 73 claims. Ranks 1, 61, 63 are different — they are nice and vague, so there is nothing to reject. For those, switch to OUI, ET: give three reasons or three concrete steps, built from the same lenses. Rank 65 is the only claim that is already balanced: agree with it and say where the line is. French connectors: YES-BUT uses certes… mais; STRIKE uses en revanche / par ailleurs; BUILD uses c'est pourquoi; SEAL uses en somme.

When you get stuck

Sometimes the page stays blank. These three tricks work on every one of the 73 claims:

1 · Start with the other side. Can't find your position? Write the "certes" sentence first — one honest sentence FOR the claim.
The "mais" will come to you while you write it. "Certes, les écrans fatiguent les yeux… mais" — and your letter has already started.
2 · Make up one plain study. The exam grades your French, not your facts. Use at most one invented study per letter, and keep it dull.
"Une étude récente estime que…" + a dull, believable number — or "En Suède, un programme similaire…"
3 · Zoom in or out. Stuck talking about all of society? Zoom in to one person (ONE FACE). Stuck on one small story? Zoom out to a whole country.
Every claim works at both levels: my street, and then the whole of France.

Worked examples

Grades should be abolished (rank 2) — BAN, two-part

"The school system is too competitive; grades should be abolished."

PREP Circle «abolished». This is a two-part claim: agree the problem is real, then argue against the fix.

STAND The problem is real, but the fix is wrong. Change how we grade — do not abolish grades.

YES-BUT Certes, the pressure of ranking is real — stress, tears over small decimals. Mais removing grades destroys information that students and parents need.

STRIKE · BACKFIRE Without grades, selection does not go away. It moves to hidden criteria — recommendation letters, interviews — and those favor students with connections.

BUILD · BETTER TOOL Grade in a better way: marks that track progress, comments next to the numbers, no public rankings. A nearby school tried this: stress went down and results held (a plain invented study).

SEAL Keep grades as a guide. Drop the public ranking.

No more books in 20 years (rank 4) — DOOM, dated prediction

"In 20 years, there will be no more books."

PREP Circle «no more». This is a dated prediction, so the ELSEWHERE lens is ready to use.

STAND Reading is changing. Books are not dying.

YES-BUT Certes, screens take up reading time, and e-book sales are rising. Mais when a format shrinks, the thing itself does not die.

STRIKE · ELSEWHERE People have predicted this before. Cinema was supposed to kill theatre. TV was supposed to kill cinema. Radio was supposed to kill newspapers. In each case, both still exist.

BUILD · BOND + ONE FACE Paper books do things a file cannot. You can give one as a gift, put it on a shelf, pass it down. My niece reads on her phone AND collects paperbacks.

SEAL In 20 years there may be fewer books. But not zero.

The oddest claims — still covered

Acceptable lies (rank 65) — VERDICT, already balanced: the only claim with "sometimes / it depends" built in. It does not exaggerate, so OUI, MAIS has nothing to push against. Switch lanes: agree, and use your three arguments to say where the line is — BOND (small white lies protect feelings) · BACKFIRE (lying as a habit destroys trust) · ONE FACE. The lenses still work. Only your position changes.

Life is short (rank 66) — CREED, about life itself: no law, no target group — fewer lenses work here than on any other claim. But enough still do: CERTES (we cannot do everything) · HEALTH (chasing every dream leads to burnout; choosing gives life meaning) · ONE FACE (the grandfather who chased one dream and did it well).

Telework for the rich? (rank 21) — VERDICT in question form: the claim already uses FAIRNESS, so your best lens is taken. In STAND, answer the question: "partly true — so open telework to more people instead of rejecting it." Then use the other nine lenses: HEALTH (less commuting, more family time) · BETTER TOOL (help pay for equipment, shared work spaces, a right to ask for telework).

Also covered: rank 7 (the one claim that pushes the other way — FAIRNESS still works, just in reverse: an age wall is an age wall) · rank 37 (the wording is messy; read "eliminate X" as a BAN on X and the usual three lenses work) · rank 73 (the same words as rank 16 — same letter, one free practice rep).
